Plastic bags, glass, metal: contaminants in bio-waste lower compost quality and cause considerable
effort in digestion and composting plants. How much of a delivered batch they make up is usually
determined by **sorting analysis** today – by hand, by sample and at considerable cost in time.

Our question: can the share of contaminants instead be estimated **automatically, by camera and AI** –
for every batch, right at delivery?

## How the AI sees bio-waste

A camera hangs above the waste stream. A segmentation model splits each image into individual objects
and assigns each to a class – organic material or contaminant. The image above shows the principle:
every detected area is marked on its own.

The marked areas give the **share of contaminants by area**: how much of the visible surface consists
of contaminants?

## Comparing with sorting analysis

An AI that nobody checks is of no use in practice. So we took our measurements alongside sorting
analyses. The comparison was with **variant 2** of the sorting: plastic and compostable bags are opened
and their contents sorted according to the material group catalogue. The result is a share of
contaminants **by weight**.

## What the data shows

The trend is clear: batches with a higher share of contaminants in the sorting analysis also show more
contaminants in the image analysis. At the same time the values scatter, and individual batches stand out.

> Area is not weight – and that is exactly the interesting question.

A thin plastic film covers a lot of area in the image but weighs almost nothing. A piece of glass is
heavy but small. Outliers such as a batch with a high share by area and a low share by weight can be
explained this way – but that is not confirmed yet.

## What comes next

The data so far comes from two test campaigns. Robust conclusions need more. Next up:

1. **Further campaigns**, to put the correlation on a broader data base.
2. **Analysis by material group**, so that light, large-area contaminants can be weighted differently.
3. **A conversion model** from area to weight shares, calibrated against the sorting analyses.

Our interim conclusion: AI image analysis does not replace sorting analysis – but it can assess every
batch, not just samples. If the correlation holds, it becomes a tool that lets plant operators detect
contaminants early and respond in a targeted way.
